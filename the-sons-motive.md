### Chapter Three
The Son’s Motive in the Work of Salvation


1. The Question Properly Stated
Why did the Son come?

Not merely:

Why did He die?

Not merely:

Why did God decide to save?

But:

What moved the eternal Son to assume flesh, to obey unto death, to endure the Cross, and to
rise as the exalted Mediator?

The answer must avoid two distortions:

   1. Anthropocentric reduction — as if the Son came primarily because humanity required
       rescue.

   2. Abstract determinism — as if the Son merely executed an impersonal decree.


The motive of the Son must be articulated in a way that preserves:

   - The freedom of God,

   - The unity of the Trinity,

   - The reality of Christ’s human obedience,

   - The depth of divine love,
   - The glory revealed at the Cross.


2. Christ as Telos — Without Diminishing the Cross
Creation exists through the Son and for the Son.

The Incarnation is not a reaction to sin.
It belongs to the eternal purpose of God.

But this must be said carefully.

The Incarnation is eternal in decree.
The Atonement is historically necessary because of sin.

In the world that actually exists — a fallen world —
 the Cross is not incidental.
 It is the decisive historical center.

The Son did not come merely to be united to creation.
He came to redeem it.

Redemption is not an afterthought in history.
It is the way the eternal purpose unfolds within a corrupted world.


3. The One Acting Subject
All redemptive acts belong to the one Person of the Son.

Not:

   - A divine nature acting alone,

   - Nor a human nature acting independently,

   - Nor two cooperating subjects.


But:

The eternal Son, acting according to both natures.
He obeys in His humanity.
He suffers in His humanity.
He dies in His humanity.

Yet it is the Son who obeys, suffers, and dies.

The humanity assumed is not an instrument.
It is not temporary.
It is not a detachable operational layer.

The Incarnation is irreversible.

The risen Christ remains the God-man.

Redemption is therefore not a phase the Son passes through.
It is the historical outworking of who He is as incarnate Lord.


4. The Son’s Obedience: More Than Execution
The Son’s obedience cannot be reduced to decretal coherence.

In eternity, the Son wills with the Father.
 In time, the incarnate Son receives the Father’s will.

Gethsemane must not be flattened.

“Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me.”

This is not theatrical alignment.
It is real human anguish.

The human will recoils from suffering.
The divine will eternally wills redemption.

In the one Person, there is no contradiction —
 but there is real obedience.

He does not merely execute the decree.
He entrusts Himself to the Father.

He learns obedience through suffering — not because He was previously disobedient, but
because obedience is now lived through pain.
This is not docetic alignment.
It is filial trust.


5. Active and Passive Obedience
The Son’s motive includes the full righteousness of His people.

He did not come only to remove guilt.
He came to fulfill righteousness.

In His life, He renders perfect obedience to the law.
 In His death, He bears the penalty of its curse.

The Cross is not a mere clearing of debt.
It is the establishment of positive righteousness.

This obedience is personal.
It is covenantal.
It is joyful — though through tears.


6. Definite Covenant Love — Without Constriction
The Son loves those given to Him by the Father.

This love is not vague possibility.
It is effective, covenantal, and saving.

He does not die merely to make salvation available.
He dies to save.

Yet Scripture speaks of “the world.”

This must not be domesticated too quickly.

The love of God is not thinly calculated affection.
It is holy, sovereign, and free.

The particularity of redemption does not negate the generosity of divine goodness.

The Son’s love is definite in accomplishment
and abundant in character.
It does not spill beyond the decree —
 but the decree itself flows from the fullness of divine love.


7. Glory — Not Output, But Radiance
Glory is not a ledger balance.

It is not the sum of attributes displayed.

Glory is the radiant fullness of God revealed and shared.

At the Cross:

Justice is not merely satisfied — it is unveiled.
 Love is not merely demonstrated — it is poured out.
 Faithfulness is not merely maintained — it is vindicated.

The Son glorifies the Father not by maximizing divine ego,
but by revealing the beauty of divine self-giving.

Glory here is weight.
It is beauty.
It is the splendor of triune love made visible in suffering.


8. The Joy Set Before Him
The Son endured the Cross for the joy set before Him.

This joy is not private gain.
It is covenant fulfillment.

It includes:

    - The vindication of obedience,

    - The gathering of the redeemed,

    - The exaltation of the incarnate Mediator,

    - The consummation of communion.
The joy is relational.

The joy is corporate.

The joy is the Father receiving a redeemed people in the Son.

He does not endure mechanically.
He endures purposefully.


9. Trinitarian Harmony
The Father does not oppose the Son.
The Son does not placate a reluctant God.

The will is one.

The Father gives.
The Son offers.
The Spirit applies.

These are ordered personal relations, not divided intentions.

The Son drinking the cup is not submission to alien wrath.
It is the ordered execution of triune love and justice.


10. Final Thesis
The Son came because, in the eternal freedom of God, creation was purposed in Him.

He entered fallen history not as Plan B,
not as detached executor,
not as temporary mediator.

He came as the incarnate Son:

    - Obedient in real human anguish,

    - Loving with definite covenant love,

    - Fulfilling righteousness fully,
   - Bearing judgment personally,

   - Revealing divine glory radiantly,

   - Enduring for the joy of redeemed communion.


The decree was free.
The obedience was personal.
The suffering was real.
The love was effective.
The glory was beautiful.

Christ is not reaction.
He is not abstraction.
He is not mechanism.

He is the incarnate Son
who loved, obeyed, suffered, trusted, and triumphed —
and remains the God-man forever.


Clarification on Voluntariness, Decree, and
the Human Obedience of Christ
(To be inserted following the section on Gethsemane and obedience)


On Freedom, Certainty, and the Human Will of Christ
The voluntariness of Christ’s obedience must be articulated with precision.

A common modern definition of freedom identifies it as the ability to do otherwise in any given
circumstance. This libertarian definition is not assumed here. It is neither required by Scripture
nor compatible with classical Christology.

Christian orthodoxy affirms that the incarnate Son could not sin. The impossibility of sin does not
render His obedience unreal. Rather, it establishes that His obedience was the unhindered
operation of a will perfectly ordered to righteousness.
Freedom, in this theological framework, is not indeterminacy. It is the uncoerced exercise of a
nature according to its inclination.

Coercion occurs when a will is compelled contrary to its disposition. In Christ, no such coercion
exists. His human will is true, complete, and capable of natural aversion to suffering. In
Gethsemane, the recoil from the cup is genuine. The anguish is real. The sorrow is profound.

Yet this recoil is not moral resistance to the Father’s will. It is the natural human aversion to
death and wrath. When the Son prays, “Not my will, but yours be done,” He does not override a
sinful inclination. He freely aligns the human will’s natural desire for life beneath the higher good
of redemptive obedience.

The certainty of the decree does not negate this obedience. Certainty is not compulsion. The
eternal decree establishes that the Son will obey; it does not force the human will against its
nature. The obedience of Christ is therefore both:

   - Eternally purposed, and

   - Personally enacted through a real human will.


The decree guarantees the outcome without simulating the experience.

In this respect, Christ’s obedience anticipates the freedom of the glorified saints. In the
consummated state, the redeemed will be unable to sin, yet perfectly free. Their confirmed
righteousness will not diminish voluntariness but perfect it. Likewise, the incarnate Son’s
impeccable will does not diminish freedom; it manifests it in its highest form.

Therefore:

The agony of Gethsemane is not theatrical.
The submission is not mechanical.
The obedience is not scripted performance.

It is the real, conscious, willing act of the incarnate Son, whose human will truly receives, truly
suffers, and truly submits — all within the sovereign certainty of the Father’s eternal purpose.


This clarification accomplishes:

   - Explicit rejection of libertarian freedom.

   - Clear compatibilist definition.
   - Direct Christological grounding.

   - Preservation of decree.

   - Preservation of real anguish.

   - Elimination of the “performance” objection.

   - Pastoral acknowledgment without structural collapse.

On Decree and Receptive Obedience
(To follow the section on Freedom and Certainty)


On Decree, Command, and the Receptivity of the Son
A further clarification is required concerning the relation between the eternal decree and the
Son’s human obedience in time.

The decree is the eternal determination of all that comes to pass within the one divine will. In
this decree, the Son wills redemption with the Father and the Spirit.

But the incarnate Son does not obey the decree as decree.

He obeys the Father’s command as command.

The distinction is essential.

The decree belongs to eternity and to the divine will as such.
The command belongs to history and to the economy of salvation.

In His human consciousness, the Son does not access or consult the eternal decree. He lives
and acts within creaturely conditions. He prays. He learns. He discerns. He entrusts Himself to
the Father.

Thus, in time:
   - The Father sends.

   - The Father commands.

   - The Son receives.

   - The Son obeys.


This reception is not theatrical.

The human will of Christ encounters the Father’s will as given — through Scripture, through the
Spirit, through providence, through the unfolding mission that leads Him toward Jerusalem.

The decree guarantees that the Son will obey.
The command is what He actually obeys.

The decree establishes certainty.
The command elicits filial response.

Therefore, the Son’s obedience is genuinely receptive.

He does not execute a script read from eternity.
He trusts the Father’s present word.
He walks toward the Cross not as one mechanically unfolding a plan, but as the incarnate Son
who believes, loves, and obeys.

The eternal will of the Son as God is not divided from His human obedience. Yet the mode of
willing differs:

   - As God, He wills redemption eternally.

   - As man, He receives and obeys the Father’s will in time.


This distinction does not introduce two subjects. It preserves the ordered relations within the
one Person acting according to two natures.

Thus:

The obedience is not simulated.
The reception is not illusory.
The filial trust is not staged.

The decree ensures that redemption will occur.
The command draws forth the obedience by which it occurs.
In this way, the Son’s obedience is at once:

   - Eternally purposed,

   - Historically commanded,

   - Personally enacted,

   - And genuinely receptive.


Now the architecture is internally complete at the key seam:

   - Compatibilism clarified.

   - Certainty distinguished from coercion.

   - Impeccability preserved.

   - Gethsemane intact.

   - Reception explained.

   - No Nestorian division introduced.

   - No libertarian concession made.


The cry is no longer an unassimilated addition.

It has a metaphysical home.
