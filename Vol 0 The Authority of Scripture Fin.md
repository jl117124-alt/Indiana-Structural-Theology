# Chapter 1

**Pre-Chapter Summary**  
**Volume 0 — Chapter 1: Revelation as Load-Bearing Redemptive Architecture**  
Before anything can be said about redemption, Scripture must be understood for what it truly is. This chapter does not begin by asking whether the Bible is inspiring, useful, or traditionally honored. It begins further down, at the level of necessity. If God’s saving work is a real work in history—ordered, mediated, and unfolding toward a fixed end—then that work must also be made knowable in a stable way.  
God did not redeem His people through vague religious memory, private insight, or symbolic approximation. He acted in history, through covenant, promise, mediation, atonement, resurrection, and final disclosure. And because He acted in this way, His redemptive work must be revealed in a way that preserves its order, its meaning, and its exclusivity.  
This means Scripture is not merely information about salvation. It is the God-given means by which the saving work of God remains coherent, transmissible, and recoverable across generations. Without revelation, the mediator could not be identified, the promises could not be traced, the meaning of the Cross could not be fixed, and the hope of final redemption would dissolve into uncertainty.  
So this chapter lays the foundation by showing that revelation is not ornamental to redemption. It is load-bearing. Without it, redemptive history would not remain navigable. The knowledge of God’s saving work would fragment, drift, and collapse into contradiction.  
Therefore, Scripture is not authoritative because the church decided to treat it that way. Scripture is authoritative because God’s redemptive work cannot remain stable, knowable, and recoverable apart from it.  
This chapter establishes the first great foundation stone: if redemption is a real and structured work of God in history, then revelation must stand as the necessary architecture that carries it.

# Chapter 2

**Pre-Chapter Summary**  
**Volume 0 — Chapter 2: Revelation Is Structurally Necessary**  
If God is truly redeeming sinners in history, then His saving work must also be made known by Him. This chapter shows that revelation is not a helpful addition to redemption, but a necessary part of how redemption can be known, received, and carried forward.  
God’s saving work is not vague, private, or self-interpreting. It unfolds through covenant, promise, mediation, fulfillment, judgment, and final hope. If God did not reveal these things, then His people could not rightly know the Savior, understand His work, or distinguish the true gospel from false religion.  
This means salvation cannot be left to human discovery, cultural memory, private insight, or natural observation. Creation may testify that God is, but it does not reveal Christ crucified and risen. Human reason may recognize need, but it does not identify the Redeemer. The gospel must be revealed.  
This chapter therefore establishes that revelation is necessary because redemption itself is structured, historical, and sequence-bound. If God’s saving work is to remain knowable, transmissible, and recoverable, then God must speak from outside us and make His work known.  
So we must reject every view that treats revelation as optional, secondary, or replaceable. Without revelation, the church would not have the truth of salvation. It would have only fragments, guesses, and competing claims.  
God speaks because His people cannot save themselves, and they also cannot discover His saving way unless He makes it known.

**Volume 0 — Chapter 3: Revelation Is a Corridor, Not Information**

Information can be collected, rearranged, interpreted, or ignored. But God has not revealed redemption in that way. He has revealed it as a living and ordered whole: promise moving toward fulfillment, covenant toward Christ, death toward resurrection, judgment toward final consummation. # Chapter 4

**Pre-Chapter Summary**  
**Volume 0 — Chapter 4: Progressive Revelation Is Ordered, Not Indefinite**  
God has not revealed His saving work all at once, but neither has He left it open without direction or end. This chapter shows that revelation unfolds in an ordered way, moving forward through promise, covenant, and fulfillment toward a fixed completion.  
From the beginning, what God reveals is not random or expandable at will. Each stage builds upon what came before. The promises given earlier are not replaced, but fulfilled. The meaning becomes clearer, the mediator becomes more fully known, and the structure of redemption becomes more defined as history moves forward.  
Because of this, we must reject every idea that treats revelation as something that can continue indefinitely, as though new stages, new mediators, or new saving acts could still be introduced. If revelation were open in that way, then nothing would ever be settled. The work of redemption would remain unfinished, and the people of God would never stand on firm ground.  
But God has revealed His work in a way that moves toward completion. What begins in promise reaches its fulfillment in Christ. As revelation progresses, it does not expand outward into new possibilities—it narrows toward a single, finished reality.  
This chapter establishes that revelation is progressive, but not indefinite. It is ordered, directed, and moving toward fulfillment.   
And this leads to the next necessary question:  
 If revelation truly moves toward completion, what event brings that completion to its final and decisive end?
